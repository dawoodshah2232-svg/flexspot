## Follow your own dollar

Imagine you hand a courier a dollar to deliver to a friend. The friend gets 36 cents. The courier network keeps 29 cents in fees. And 35 cents gets spent on a photocopy of the friend that gets delivered to an empty room. You'd never use that courier again.

That's programmatic advertising. We know because the industry finally let accountants watch the money move.

In December 2023, the Association of National Advertisers published the second part of its Programmatic Media Supply Chain Transparency Study — the most serious attempt ever made to follow an ad dollar from an advertiser's hand to a human eyeball. The researchers tracked $123 million in real campaign spend across 21 big marketers — names like State Farm, Mondelez, and Dell — from September 2022 to January 2023, tracing 35.5 billion impressions through the machinery. What came out the other end was sobering.

## The 36-cent dollar

Here's the breakdown, per the ANA: for every dollar that enters a demand-side platform (DSP) — the software most programmatic ads are bought through — only 36 cents effectively reaches a consumer.

Twenty-nine cents goes to transaction costs: DSP fees, SSP fees, and the other intermediaries that clip a little off each hop. Thirty-five cents goes to what the study calls low-quality media: impressions served to bots (invalid traffic), ads on "made-for-advertising" sites built purely to harvest ad impressions, ads nobody could see, and ads that couldn't even be measured. Extrapolated across the open web's $88 billion in programmatic spend, the ANA estimates $22 billion is wasted or unproductive. Not underperforming — gone.

Two details make it worse. First, the study found little correlation between what advertisers paid and the quality of what they got. The whole premise of paying premium prices for premium inventory didn't hold: expensive impressions were no more likely to be real, viewable, and human. Second, one supply-side platform in the study was taking close to 45% of the media spend passing through it — while most of its peers took under 5%. Same product category, ten times the toll. And the advertisers couldn't see it, because nobody shows them the whole chain.

Worth noting: the biggest DSPs in the business — Google's DV360, The Trade Desk, and Amazon's DSP — declined to participate in the study. The results we have come from the vendors willing to open their books.

## The 15% that vanished

The ANA wasn't even the first to go looking. In 2020, the UK's Incorporated Society of British Advertisers hired PwC to map programmatic supply chains end to end — from advertiser to publisher — for the first time anywhere. They pulled real market data from 15 advertisers, 8 agencies, 5 DSPs, 6 SSPs, and 12 publishers, covering about £100 million in UK spend and over a billion impressions.

PwC found that only 51% of advertiser spend ever reached publishers. The rest went to supply-chain costs — and 15% of the total fell into what they called the "unknown delta": money that left the advertiser but could not be linked to any part of the supply chain at all. No proof the ads were delivered. No proof they were viewed. Just... gone. The study noted that only about 12% of impressions could even be matched end to end, because data quality across the chain was so poor.

A 2022 follow-up found progress — the unknown delta fell to 3%, and publishers' share rose to 65% — which proves the industry *can* clean this up when it decides to. But note where the floor is: even in the improved study, a third of every dollar never reached the publisher, let alone the reader.

## The junk factory: made-for-advertising sites

Where does the 35 cents of "low-quality media" actually go? A large share goes to made-for-advertising (MFA) sites — websites designed not to be read, but to be auctioned. Slideshows stretched across 40 pages, clickbait galleries, auto-refreshing ad slots, articles engineered so the ads load and the content barely matters. They're built to be sold into programmatic auctions, and they're remarkably good at it: the ANA found MFA supply grew from about 5% of web auctions in early 2020 to nearly 30% by mid-2023. Almost a third of the auctions your money flows into are auctions on sites nobody visits on purpose.

The reason MFA thrives is the chain itself. When the average advertiser in the ANA study was running campaigns across an astonishing 44,000 websites and apps, routed through an average of 19 different SSPs, nobody is curating placements. The machines buy whatever is cheap and available, and MFA sites are always available. The ANA's recommendation: cap your SSPs at seven, and only use SSPs that give you log-level transparency into where your money went. Nineteen middlemen, it turns out, is not diversification — it's 19 places for your dollar to leak.

## What this means for a small budget

Here's the uncomfortable part: the studies above are about big brands with media teams and auditors. If State Farm's dollar arrives at 36 cents, your hundred dollars faces the same machinery with none of the oversight. Small advertisers can't negotiate log-level data, can't demand fee caps, and can't audit a supply chain with dozens of invisible links. You get a dashboard, a CPM, and a number for clicks.

But the same studies point at the way out, and it's refreshingly simple: the waste lives in the chain, so shorten the chain.

Every hop between you and the placement is a toll booth you can't see inside. A DSP takes its cut. An SSP takes its cut. A verification vendor, a data vendor, a reseller each take theirs. One platform took 45%. The alternative isn't better targeting or a smarter algorithm — it's buying where the chain is one link long: advertiser bids, placement sells, one fee you can see, done.

That was the original promise of the auction. We've argued before that [auctions are the fairest way to buy attention](/blog/auctions-fairest-way-to-buy-attention) and shown [how auction rules quietly decide what ads cost](/blog/first-price-vs-second-price-auctions). The ad tech tax doesn't disprove that argument — it explains why fairness needs visibility. An auction is only fair if you can see the bids, the rules, and the take. The ANA's 36-cent dollar is what happens when you can see none of them.

## The small-budget playbook

**Count the links.** Before you put money anywhere, ask how many companies sit between your payment and the person who sees your ad. If nobody can answer, the answer is "too many."

**Price quality, not just cost.** The ANA's finding that price and quality barely correlate is an opportunity: it means cheap, direct inventory with real humans can outperform expensive programmatic buys. Buy where you can name the website your ad appears on.

**Treat "unknown" as a cost.** The ISBA's unknown delta — spend with no proof of delivery — exists at every scale. Any channel that can't show you where the ad appeared, to a human, is charging you for the unknown delta too.

**Use visible auctions.** A public auction with visible bids, a published reserve, and a human verifying each bid has a supply chain of one link. There's nowhere for a 45% toll or a mystery delta to hide.

The advertising industry's dirty secret isn't that ads don't work. It's that the machinery built to sell them takes most of the money before the ad ever loads. The brands that figured this out first were the big ones with auditors. The small ones have an advantage the big ones don't: they can simply choose not to use the machine. A short, visible chain beats a "sophisticated" one — and you don't need a $123 million audit to prove it.
