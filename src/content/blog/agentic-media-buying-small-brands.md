## Thursday was a quiet milestone for small advertisers

On October 1, Kargo — an advertising platform founded back in 2003 — announced the general availability of Karlo, a self-serve agentic media buying platform built specifically for smaller teams and businesses. The pitch, per the company's press release: campaigns planned, created, and activated across the open web, connected TV, and social from one system, set up in minutes, with all reporting in one place. It had been in closed beta earlier this year under the name Project KERA.

The interesting part isn't that another AI tool exists. It's what Karlo is designed to replace: not the advertiser, but the *operator* — the person who used to translate a brief into audiences, placements, bids, and a creative plan spread across five different dashboards.

## What Karlo actually does

Karlo runs on two things Kargo has spent two decades building. First, "Creative Science®" — Kargo's proprietary performance data on how creative behaves across platforms. Second, "Media Intelligence" — premium audience data combined with Kargo's contextual signals. The company's tagline for the product is "One Platform. Every Screen."

You start with an existing briefing document or a natural-language prompt describing your goals and audience. The agent then scores your creative alongside targeting and bidding decisions, in real time — and keeps adjusting once the campaign is live.

Kargo's founder and CEO Harry Kargman put the thesis bluntly: most agentic tools launching right now are built around bidding and targeting — functions that were, in his words, already pretty well-automated. Creative usually sits outside that loop, untouched, even though it's the biggest lever on performance. Karlo takes the creative a business already has and folds it into decision-making, scoring, and adjustments, alongside targeting and bidding, in real time.

For its launch-day evidence, Kargo pointed to an Ad Council campaign for the "Love, Your Mind" mental health initiative run through Karlo, which the company said drove a 21% lift in ad recall and a 10% lift in aided awareness. That's Kargo's own number from a partner campaign, not an independent study — worth keeping in mind before you take it as a benchmark.

## Why this matters more than another product launch

For years, the story of programmatic advertising was about *access*: auction-based buying gave any advertiser with a credit card the theoretical right to bid for attention anywhere on the internet. In practice, though, the machinery stayed hard to run well. Small brands had access to the auction house but couldn't afford the auctioneer.

Agentic buying flips that. An industry briefing this week put it neatly: programmatic democratised access to inventory; agents could democratise operation of the machinery. Instead of the old prompt — "write me five ads" — the new one reads: "here is my budget, my customer, and my business objective. Help me decide how to spend it."

Think about what that prompt hands over: where to advertise, who to target, which creative to use, how to allocate spend, what to adjust mid-flight. A small brand owner who previously had to hire an agency or learn five platforms can now, in theory, brief the way they'd brief a sharp media buyer — and get a running campaign back.

## The uncomfortable part: the auction is getting faster than you

There's a flip side worth naming. When everyone has an agent bidding on their behalf, the auctions for attention get smarter, denser, and harder to game with clever manual tricks. Your competitive edge stops being *knowledge of the machinery* — the agent has that — and moves up the stack: your positioning, your creative insight, your understanding of unit economics, and your willingness to measure honestly.

That's the part of the briefing I keep coming back to: "AI is commoditising marketing execution faster than marketing judgment." Knowing which buttons to click inside an ad platform is becoming less valuable than knowing what deserves to be advertised in the first place.

## What a small brand should do this month

Karlo's launch is one product, not a guarantee. Kargo hasn't published pricing or minimums at the time of writing, and one vendor's launch-day case study is not proof of anything about your budget. But the direction is clear enough to act on:

**Keep building creative that is worth scoring.** If agents now evaluate creative as part of bidding, the fundamentals still win: a clear offer, a genuine reason to care, something that stops a thumb. An agent can distribute good creative; it can't invent your point of view.

**Protect your measurement.** Agents optimise toward whatever you tell them to optimise. If you set a vanity target, you'll get vanity performance at machine speed. Define what a customer is actually worth to you before you let anything bid on your behalf.

**Stay close to the auction.** The attention markets we've covered in this blog — [first-price versus second-price auctions](/blog/first-price-vs-second-price-auctions), the [ad tech tax](/blog/ad-tech-tax-where-your-ad-dollar-goes) — are about to be run partly by machines on both sides. Understanding how bidding works doesn't become *less* important when an agent does it for you; it becomes the vocabulary you use to check its work.

**Treat agentic buying as a lever, not a replacement.** The best use of a tool like Karlo is the one its own maker implies: small teams get enterprise-grade execution, but the strategy still comes from someone who understands the business. If you wouldn't trust an agency that never asked about your margins, don't trust an agent that never does either.

## The bottom line

Two decades ago, programmatic auctions opened the door to every ad impression on the internet. This week, agentic platforms started handing out the expertise to bid in them well. For small brands, that's genuinely good news — a capable media buyer in a box costs less than a media buyer on payroll.

But attention was never won by the best machinery alone. It was won by the brand with the clearest reason to be noticed. The agents are here to do the buying. The being worth buying still falls to you.
