> **Key takeaways:**
> - Building in public means sharing the journey — wins, numbers, mistakes — while you build, not after.
> - It works because people root for people: a founder's story earns attention that a product page never will.
> - Share process and lessons freely; keep exact finances, unlaunched features, and customer data private.
> - Consistency beats virality — a weekly update for six months outperforms one viral thread.
> - Your public archive becomes launch fuel: every follower is a day-one visitor when you ship.

## What "building in public" actually means

Building in public is the practice of sharing your startup journey openly as it happens: the idea, the progress, the revenue numbers (if you're comfortable), the mistakes, the pivots. Not a polished announcement after the fact — the messy middle, in real time.

It works for a simple psychological reason: people don't share product pages, but they do follow stories. A founder posting "week 6: we finally fixed onboarding, activation up" gives followers a narrative to root for. By launch day, those followers aren't cold traffic — they're invested.

## What to share

### The process

"What I built this week" updates are the backbone. Screenshots of progress, short videos of new features, before-and-after comparisons. Process content is easy to produce (you're doing the work anyway) and it compounds — week 20 of updates is a story no competitor can copy.

### The numbers

Revenue, users, conversion rates — shared numbers are the highest-trust content in the build-in-public world. They're also the scariest to post. You don't have to share everything: pick the metrics you're comfortable with and be consistent about them. "MRR" every month beats a one-time screenshot.

### The mistakes

"I shipped a pricing page that cut conversions in half" will outperform any success post you write. Failure posts get shared because they're useful and rare — most founders only post wins, so honest post-mortems stand out. They also make your wins believable.

### The lessons

Every mistake is a lesson post. Every decision is a framework post. "Why we chose X over Y" content positions you as a thoughtful founder and attracts exactly the kind of early adopters who care about craft.

## What to keep private

Building in public is not building naked. Keep these off the timeline:

- **Exact financials you're not comfortable defending** — share ranges or percentages instead.
- **Unlaunched features with real novelty** — talk about the problem you're solving, not the proprietary mechanism, until it's shipped.
- **Customer data** — never share anything identifiable about users without explicit permission.
- **Fundraising details** — terms, valuations, and investor conversations stay private until closed and announced.
- **Team conflicts** — the messy human stuff stays in-house. Authenticity has a line, and it's here.

## The cadence that works

Forget virality as a strategy. The pattern that actually builds audiences:

1. **Pick one primary channel** where your future customers already hang out. For B2B SaaS that's usually X/Twitter or LinkedIn; for consumer, it might be TikTok or Instagram.
2. **Post on a schedule you can sustain for a year.** Weekly deep updates beat daily low-effort posts. The algorithm rewards consistency more than intensity.
3. **Use a repeatable format.** "Week N: shipped / learned / next" is boring and perfect — followers learn to expect it and check in.
4. **Engage, don't just broadcast.** Reply to comments, comment on other builders' updates, be a citizen of the community. Audiences are built in replies as much as in posts.

## Turning the audience into a launch

When launch day comes, your public archive does the heavy lifting:

- Announce the launch where you've been building — your followers are warm traffic with context.
- Quote your own journey: "6 months ago I posted the first sketch — today it's live" is a story with a built-in emotional arc.
- Ask your audience to show up, not just to cheer. Give them the link, the ask, and a reason (early access, founding pricing, a thank-you in the credits).

Founders who build in public don't launch to strangers. They launch to people who've been watching the whole season — and those people show up.
