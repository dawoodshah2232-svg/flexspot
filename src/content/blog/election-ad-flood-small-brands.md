## A familiar story, now with a $11.6 billion twist

Every election cycle, advertisers hear the same warning: political money is coming, and your ad prices are about to jump. This year's version has numbers worth taking seriously. AdImpact projects $11.6 billion in political ad spending for the 2026 cycle — surpassing both the $8.9 billion spent in the 2022 midterms and the $11.2 billion spent in the 2024 presidential cycle. For the first time, a midterm is on track to outspend the presidential election that came before it.

This is a US story, but the mechanics travel. If you buy any of your attention in the United States — and many small digital brands do — the next four weeks will move your prices. And even if you don't, the playbook below works for any demand shock in an auction market: holiday retail surges, major sports events, a competitor flooding your niche. Auction prices respond to demand, not to your budget.

## Where the money is landing

Roughly half the political spend — about $5.6 billion, or 48% — is going to broadcast television. That's the familiar headline. The less familiar one is connected TV: AdImpact projects around $2.6–2.7 billion in political CTV and streaming spend, about 23% of the total, with another $1.6 billion headed to digital platforms and $1.4 billion to cable.

BIA Advisory Services tells the local version of the same story. In its September update, BIA raised the 2026 local political forecast to $9.7 billion, up from $8.4 billion in April — and $1.2 billion of that $1.3 billion increase flowed into TV over-the-air and TV OTT, broadcast and streaming video together.

One data point from a media-buying briefing last week captures the pace: through late August, roughly $6.1 billion in political advertising had already been placed across media. The 60-day window that matters most — the stretch before the November 3 general election — is now fully underway.

## Why this hits commercial advertisers harder than it looks

Federal law requires broadcast stations to sell airtime to legally qualified candidates at their lowest unit charge during the 60 days before a general election. For this election, that window opened September 4. Worse for everyone else's wallet, the US Supreme Court reinstated FCC guidance extending those lowest rates to certain party ads coordinated with candidates and to joint fundraising committees.

So a large chunk of that $11.6 billion now qualifies for a station's best pricing, chasing a fixed number of spots. There are only so many thirty-second slots in a 6 p.m. newscast. When political demand spikes, commercial advertisers feel it three ways: higher rates, less available inventory, and more preemptions — your paid spot bumped by a candidate's.

Note the asymmetry: the lowest-unit-charge protection covers candidates, not you. PACs, issue campaigns, and state and local races pay market rates too. Industry reporting puts the October effect bluntly — media firm Assembly told advertisers in high-intensity markets to prepare for all three, and Scale Marketing expects October to be the peak of the squeeze, with news and sports programming hit hardest. Exactly where many local businesses like to be seen.

One more comfort to lose: streaming is not the hiding place it used to be. Commercial advertisers have long treated CTV as the escape route when linear inventory gets crowded. In 2026, political buyers are using CTV for the same reasons you do — precise audience targeting, geographic precision, and cord-cutters — so that assumption deserves a hard look.

## What the price shock actually looks like

Rates in the final weeks before an election can run 200–300% above baseline, according to 2026 cost guides — with the caveat that federal candidates are partly shielded by the lowest-unit rules while everyone else absorbs the market.

For context on the normal ranges this shock disrupts: local broadcast spots typically run $100–$500 off-peak and $500–$2,500 in prime; CTV CPMs run $25–$45 on premium inventory, $18–$30 on mid-tier, and $12–$22 on free ad-supported channels. When billions of dollars chase the same households, those are the floors, not the ceilings.

## The playbook for small brands this October

None of this means going dark for a month. That would be the most expensive option — rebuilding momentum after four silent weeks costs more than paying a temporary premium. Here's how to keep buying attention while the flood is on.

**Lock what matters now, in writing.** If you have broadcast or cable placements that are performing, confirm them with your rep today. Preemptions are legal and routine in October; a handshake won't protect a spot the way a firm order with preemption protection does. Ask specifically what your makegood terms look like.

**Shift time and inventory, not just budget.** Early morning and late night on local news can cost 60–80% less than the same station's prime time. Political money concentrates on news and sports, so lifestyle, entertainment, and off-peak dayparts feel less pressure. On CTV, free ad-supported channels carry meaningful political dollars too — but mid-tier and self-serve inventory still clears far below premium.

**Use CTV self-serve with zip-level targeting.** This is the structural advantage small advertisers have: you can buy a single zip code or district and ignore the statewide race driving up your market. Self-serve CTV platforms let campaigns start from as little as $50, so the barrier isn't the minimum — it's the CPM. Geographic precision is how you dodge a bidding war you were never going to win.

**Move a share of budget to channels auctions can't price-gouge.** Creator and influencer spend is negotiated, not auctioned. Email and SMS reach an audience you already own. Retail media and local sponsorships clear outside the TV market entirely. October is a good month to overweight the channels where your spend is a fixed cost, not a bid.

**Shorten your measurement window.** Political pressure makes week-over-week CPM comparisons noisy. Measure on outcomes — cost per lead, cost per store visit, cost per conversion — and compare against your own baseline from August, not against the CPM a station quotes you on a chaotic October day.

**Don't chase the flood upward.** The one move that always loses: increasing bids to "stay competitive" in an auction dominated by bidders who aren't optimising for return. Candidates optimise for votes by a fixed date. You optimise for profit. Different game — play yours.

## The bottom line

This October is a live demonstration of the thing this blog keeps coming back to: [attention is bought at auction](/blog/auctions-fairest-way-to-buy-attention), and auctions respond to demand. $11.6 billion of political demand, much of it entitled to the lowest published rates while you pay market price, is the heaviest demand shock the US ad market has seen in a midterm cycle.

The brands that come through it well aren't the ones with the biggest budgets. They're the ones who understand the machinery — [how first-price and second-price auctions actually clear](/blog/first-price-vs-second-price-auctions), [where the ad tech tax eats their dollar](/blog/ad-tech-tax-where-your-ad-dollar-goes) — and adjust before the peak instead of reacting at it. The flood passes on November 4. How much of your budget it carried away is up to you.
