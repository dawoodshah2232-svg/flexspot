> **Key takeaways:**
> - Google Ads captures existing demand (people searching to buy); Facebook/Meta Ads creates demand (interrupting scrollers with something appealing).
> - On small budgets, the winner depends on your business: urgent needs and specific searches favor Google; visual, impulse, and local offers favor Meta.
> - Splitting a tiny budget across both platforms usually means neither gets enough data to learn — pick one primary.
> - Whichever you choose, the landing page decides the outcome more than the platform does.

## Two different machines

Facebook (Meta) Ads and Google Ads are often compared as if they're two versions of the same thing. They're not — they're opposite machines:

- **Google Ads** shows your ad to people **already looking** for what you sell. Someone searches "emergency plumber near me" and your ad appears. This is *intent capture*.
- **Meta Ads** (Facebook + Instagram) shows your ad to people **based on who they are**, while they're scrolling for entertainment. Nobody was looking for your product. This is *interruption* — you have to earn the attention.

Neither is universally better. They're good at different jobs, and on a small budget, picking the wrong machine for your job is the most expensive mistake you can make.

## How they differ on a small budget

### Cost per click and per customer

Google search clicks generally cost more than Meta clicks, because the intent is higher — advertisers bid aggressively for people about to buy. Meta clicks are cheaper because the audience wasn't shopping. But cost per click is the wrong metric; **cost per customer** is what matters. Expensive Google clicks that convert can be cheaper per customer than cheap Meta clicks that don't. Track to the sale, not the click.

### Minimum viable budget

Both platforms will happily take $5 a day. The real question is whether that budget buys enough conversions for the platform's optimization to learn. A campaign that gets two conversions a month can't optimize — the algorithm has no signal. On very small budgets, simpler campaign structures (one audience, one ad set, one offer) outperform sophisticated setups because they concentrate your limited data.

### Creative demands

Meta is a creative-hungry platform: you need images or video that stop thumbs, and fresh creative regularly as audiences fatigue. If you can't produce decent visuals, Meta will punish you. Google search ads are text-led and far less creative-intensive — a well-written text ad and a good landing page can compete without any design skills.

### Targeting precision

Google targets by *search term* — you know exactly what the person wants in that moment. Meta targets by *demographics, interests, and behaviors* — you know who they are, not what they want right now. For niche B2B or unusual products, Meta's interest targeting can find audiences Google's keywords can't describe. For urgent services, nothing beats catching the exact search.

### Learning curve and management

Google Ads rewards keyword discipline: match types, negative keywords, and search-term reviews. Meta rewards creative testing and audience iteration. Both punish neglect — campaigns left unmanaged decay as audiences fatigue and competitors adjust. Budget at least a couple of hours a week for management, whichever you pick.

## Which should you choose? A decision framework

**Choose Google Ads first if:**

- People search for what you sell with buying intent ("buy," "near me," "price," "hire," "emergency").
- Your service is urgent or need-driven: plumbers, locksmiths, lawyers, repair services, clinics.
- You sell something specific that's hard to describe visually but easy to search.
- Your average sale is large enough to absorb higher click costs (one converted customer covers many clicks).

**Choose Meta Ads first if:**

- Your product is visual, novel, or impulse-friendly: fashion, food, beauty, home decor, gadgets, experiences.
- Nobody searches for what you sell because they don't know it exists yet — you need to create the desire.
- You're local and want to reach everyone in an area cheaply (restaurants, gyms, salons, events).
- You can produce scroll-stopping images or short video consistently.

**Consider neither (yet) if:**

- Your website doesn't convert — fix the page before buying traffic ([conversion guide](/blog/how-to-turn-website-traffic-into-customers)).
- You can't afford to lose your test budget while learning. Paid ads are tuition; the first campaigns teach, they rarely profit.

## How to run each on a small budget

### Small-budget Google Ads playbook

1. **Search campaigns only** — skip Display and broad formats until search works.
2. **Tight keyword lists.** Ten highly relevant keywords beat a hundred loose ones. Use phrase and exact match; review search terms weekly and add negatives relentlessly.
3. **Location and schedule limits.** Only show ads where you serve and when you can respond — paying for 3 a.m. clicks you can't answer is waste.
4. **One landing page per intent.** The ad promising "emergency AC repair pricing" must land on a page about emergency AC repair pricing, not your homepage.
5. **Call tracking.** For service businesses, calls are the conversion — make sure you count them.

### Small-budget Meta Ads playbook

1. **One campaign, one audience to start.** Broad local targeting often beats hyper-narrow interests on small budgets (more data, lower costs).
2. **Creative is the targeting.** On Meta, your image/video decides who stops. Test 3–4 creative variants before you test audiences.
3. **Simple offers.** "20% off this week" or a clear lead magnet outperforms clever branding when every dollar counts.
4. **Retargeting second.** Once your pixel has data, retargeting site visitors is usually your cheapest Meta traffic — set up the pixel from day one even before you advertise.
5. **Watch frequency.** When the same people see your ad too often, costs rise and annoyance grows. Refresh creative or expand audience.

## The honest math of small budgets

A small budget works when the numbers work. Before spending, estimate:

- **Expected clicks** = budget ÷ typical cost per click in your niche.
- **Expected conversions** = clicks × your landing page conversion rate (use your site's actual rate; if unknown, assume it's low until proven).
- **Expected revenue** = conversions × average sale value.

If expected revenue doesn't comfortably exceed spend, either the channel is wrong for your economics or your conversion rate needs work first. This arithmetic — done honestly before spending — prevents most small-budget disasters. ## Common mistakes on each platform

- **Google:** bidding on your own brand name unnecessarily (you usually rank organically anyway); ignoring negative keywords (paying for irrelevant searches); no conversion tracking; sending traffic to the homepage.
- **Meta:** boosting posts instead of using Ads Manager (fewer controls, worse targeting); targeting too narrow (starving the algorithm of data); running one creative until fatigue kills it; optimizing for clicks or engagement instead of actual conversions.
- **Both:** splitting a $150/month budget across two platforms and three campaigns — none gets enough signal to learn anything.

## The bottom line

Google Ads buys you people already looking. Meta Ads buys you attention you must convert into interest. On a small budget, the right choice is the one matching how your customers actually buy: search-driven needs go to Google, discovery-driven desires go to Meta. Pick one, fund it enough to learn, track to the sale — and only add the second platform once the first earns its keep. ## Three budget-split scenarios

How the choice plays out depends on your situation. Three common ones:

**Scenario A: Local service business, $300/month.** A plumber, salon, or repair shop. Almost always Google first: allocate the full budget to a tight search campaign on high-intent local keywords. Meta can wait — nobody impulse-buys emergency plumbing from a feed. Add Meta later for retargeting site visitors once the pixel has data.

**Scenario B: Online boutique, $300/month.** Visual products, giftable, impulse-friendly. Meta first: one campaign, strong product photography or short video, broad-ish targeting, a clear offer. Use a small slice (10–15%) for Google Shopping or branded search to capture people who saw the Meta ad and searched the brand name later — this "assist" pattern is common and worth covering.

**Scenario C: B2B service, $500/month.** Niche audience, high sale value, long cycle. Google first on specific problem keywords ("fractional CFO for startups"), because B2B buyers search when they have budget and intent. Meta second for retargeting and for reaching the same buyers with thought-leadership content that nurtures them over the long cycle.

The pattern: lead with the platform matching the buying behavior, keep the other in a supporting role (usually retargeting or branded search), and never split so thin that neither campaign learns.

## Creative testing on a shoestring

Whichever platform you pick, testing is how small budgets win against bigger spenders. The discipline:

- **Test one variable at a time.** New headline against the control, not a completely new ad — otherwise you won't know what caused the change.
- **Give tests a fair sample.** Ending a test after 50 impressions tells you nothing. Let each variant accumulate enough clicks to separate signal from noise before judging.
- **Keep a swipe file of winners.** Every test produces learning: save the winning headlines, images, and angles. Over months, this becomes a creative playbook that's genuinely hard for competitors to replicate.
- **Kill fast, scale deliberately.** Losers get paused quickly; winners get budget increases gradually (sudden big budget jumps can destabilize campaign learning — scale in steps).

## When to add the second platform

Resist adding the second platform until the first clears three bars: it's consistently profitable (or hitting your target cost per acquisition), you've scaled it as far as sensible budget increases allow, and you have the management time for another channel. Adding platforms is how small advertisers turn one working campaign into two neglected ones. Depth beats breadth until depth is maxed out.

## The hidden costs nobody puts in the comparison

Platform choice isn't just media cost — factor the full picture:

- **Management time.** Both platforms need weekly attention: checking search terms, refreshing creative, reviewing performance. Budget 2–4 hours a week per active platform. Two half-managed platforms lose to one well-managed one.
- **Creative production.** Meta's appetite for fresh creative is a real ongoing cost — either your time filming and editing, or money paid to creators. Google's text-led format is cheaper to sustain. If you can't produce new creative monthly, that alone can decide for Google.
- **Landing page work.** Both platforms punish bad landing pages, but the fix costs the same regardless — treat it as a prerequisite investment, not a platform differentiator. (See the [conversion guide](/blog/how-to-turn-website-traffic-into-customers).)
- **Learning period waste.** Every new campaign spends money while the algorithm learns — expect the first 1–2 weeks to underperform while data accumulates. This "tuition" applies per platform, another reason not to launch both simultaneously on a small budget.
- **Attribution blind spots.** Meta's reported conversions and Google's reported conversions both over-claim when a customer touches both — each platform credits itself. Use your own analytics and, ultimately, total revenue as the referee, not either platform's dashboard.

## A note on the duopoly's alternatives

Google and Meta dominate small-business ad spend, but they're not the only game — and for some businesses, not the best first move:

- **TikTok ads** suit visual, youthful products with phone-shot creative ([small-budget TikTok guide](/blog/how-to-advertise-on-tiktok-with-a-small-budget)).
- **Niche placements** — newsletters, podcasts, community sponsorships — buy trust that interrupts can't ([newsletter](/blog/newsletter-sponsorships-for-small-brands) and [podcast](/blog/podcast-sponsorships-on-a-small-budget) guides).
- **Bid-based homepage placements** let you buy visibility from $1 with no auction complexity ([how it works](/blog/what-is-bid-for-attention-marketing)).

The right question was never "Facebook or Google." It's "where does my customer's attention live, and what's the cheapest trustworthy way to earn it?" Answer that honestly, and the platform picks itself.

## Final checklist before you launch

- [ ] Landing page converts (tested on a phone, one clear CTA, proof visible)
- [ ] Conversion tracking installed and tested with a trial action
- [ ] One platform chosen based on buying behavior, not hype
- [ ] One campaign, tight targeting, daily budget cap set
- [ ] 2–3 creative or keyword variants ready for testing
- [ ] Calendar reminder: review performance weekly, judge after 2–3 weeks minimum
- [ ] Walk-away criteria defined: what results would make you pause and rethink?

Run through this list and your small budget stops being a gamble — it becomes an experiment with a hypothesis, a measurement plan, and a decision at the end. That's all advertising ever is, at any budget.
