> **Key takeaways:**
> - Journalists need stories, not ads — pitch something newsworthy (a first, a milestone, a trend, a human story), not "we exist."
> - Local media is far more accessible than national: community papers, local blogs, and neighborhood publications actively want local business stories.
> - A good pitch is short, specific, and makes the journalist's job easy: the angle, why now, who's involved, and great visuals.
> - One feature compounds: press logos become trust signals on your site, and coverage gets referenced by others.

## Why local press beats paid ads for credibility

Nobody believes your ad — but people believe a journalist's story about you. Earned media (coverage you didn't pay for) carries third-party credibility that advertising can't buy, and local media is the most accessible form of it. Community newspapers, city magazines, local blogs, neighborhood social accounts, and regional TV segments all need a constant supply of local stories. Your business can be one of them.

Press coverage also feeds everything else: it becomes a trust badge on your website, content for social media, a [visibility asset](/blog/how-to-measure-brand-visibility) that ranks in search, and often the trigger for more coverage. One good feature has a long tail.

## What journalists actually want

Forget what you want to say about your business. Journalists want **stories** — specifically:

- **Something new.** A launch, an opening, a first-of-its-kind offering, an expansion. "New" is the easiest pitch in existence.
- **A milestone.** 100th employee, 10th anniversary, 10,000th customer, a major contract. Numbers make stories concrete.
- **A human story.** The founder's unusual journey, an employee's remarkable background, a customer whose life changed. People remember people.
- **A trend angle.** Your business as an example of something bigger: "How Dubai's home bakeries are going professional," "The return of repair shops." Journalists love illustrating trends with real businesses.
- **Community impact.** Hiring locally, supporting a cause, revitalizing a street, free services for a community. Genuine beats performative — don't manufacture charity for PR.
- **Data or a strong opinion.** A survey of your customers, an unusual observation from your trade, a contrarian take on industry news. Founders with [real opinions](/blog/founder-led-marketing-for-small-business) get quoted.

What journalists don't want: "We're a great company, please write about us." That's an ad, and ads are sold by a different department.

## Finding the right people to pitch

Don't blast a press release to a generic inbox. Target precisely:

1. **Identify 5–10 relevant journalists or outlets.** Local business reporters, community editors, neighborhood bloggers, local podcast hosts. People who have actually covered businesses like yours.
2. **Read their recent work.** Reference a specific story they wrote. This single step puts you ahead of most pitchers.
3. **Find direct contacts.** Most journalists list emails publicly or respond to professional social messages. A personal email beats a press-release distribution service for local pitches.
4. **Time it right.** Pitch in the morning, early in the week, and tie to timing when possible (an opening next month, a seasonal angle, a trend in the news).

## Writing a pitch that gets opened

Keep it brutally short. Journalists skim dozens of pitches daily. Structure:

**Subject line:** The story, not your company. "Dubai's first repair café opens in Alserkal — run by a former engineer" beats "Press release: New business opening."

**Body (under 150 words):**
- One line on why you're writing *them* specifically ("loved your piece on…").
- The story in two sentences: what's happening, why it's interesting now.
- Who's available for quotes/photos and when.
- Offer visuals: journalists are desperate for good photos. Offer professional-quality images or an invitation to shoot on-site.

**Close** with your phone number and one-line company description. No attachments on first contact (they get flagged); link to a simple press page or a shared folder instead.

Then follow up **once**, politely, after 4–5 days. No response after that means no — move on, don't pester.

## Making yourself pitchable year-round

The businesses that get covered repeatedly maintain a few assets:

- **A simple press page** on your website: your story in a paragraph, founder bio, high-resolution photos and logo, contact details. When a journalist is interested, this removes all friction.
- **A running list of story angles.** Keep a note of milestones, unusual customers, interesting data from your business, and trend observations. When news breaks in your industry, check the list — you might be the perfect example.
- **Availability and speed.** Journalists work on deadlines measured in hours. The business that picks up the phone and provides a quote today beats the more interesting business that replies next week.
- **Visual readiness.** Good photos of your premises, products, team, and founder — ready to send. Visual stories need visuals.

## Beyond traditional press

"Media" is wider than newspapers:

- **Local bloggers and newsletter writers** often have more engaged local audiences than legacy outlets — and they're easier to reach.
- **Podcasts** (local business shows, industry podcasts) need guests constantly. A founder with opinions is an easy yes.
- **Community social accounts** — neighborhood Instagram pages, city guides, local TikTok creators — function as media for younger audiences. Treat them with the same professional pitching approach.
- **Awards and lists.** "Best of" lists, local business awards, industry rankings — apply for them. Even being shortlisted is a credential you can display.

## After the coverage lands

Don't just celebrate — extract full value:

1. **Display it.** "As featured in…" with outlet logos on your homepage is a permanent trust signal.
2. **Share it everywhere** — social, email list, WhatsApp status. Most businesses under-share their own press.
3. **Thank the journalist** genuinely, and stay in touch. A good relationship means they'll call you next time they need a source in your industry.
4. **Link it in future pitches.** "Recently featured in X" makes the next journalist's decision easier — coverage compounds.

Local media won't make you famous overnight. But a steady drumbeat of local features builds the kind of credible, compounding [visibility](/blog/how-to-increase-brand-visibility) that ads can't replicate — and it costs nothing but thoughtfulness and follow-through.

## Following up without being annoying

The follow-up is where pitches live or die. Rules:

- **One follow-up, 4–5 days later.** Short: "Just floating this back up in case it's useful — happy to provide quotes or photos on your timeline." Then stop.
- **Add value in the follow-up, don't just nag.** New information helps: "Since I wrote, we've [new development]" or "I saw your piece on [related topic] — this might make a good follow-up angle."
- **Accept silence as no.** Journalists are drowning in pitches. No reply after one follow-up means pass — pitching the same person the same story again burns the relationship.
- **Keep a pitch log.** Who you pitched, when, what angle, what happened. It prevents embarrassing double-pitches and shows you which angles get bites over time.
- **Stay warm regardless.** Engage with their work genuinely (thoughtful comments, shares) outside of pitching. The relationship you build between pitches determines whether the next pitch gets opened.

## Start this week: your first-pitch action list

1. List 5 local outlets or journalists who cover businesses like yours.
2. Read one recent story from each and note the angle they favor.
3. Pick your strongest story angle from the list in this guide (a launch, milestone, human story, or trend tie-in).
4. Draft your 150-word pitch and prepare 3–5 good photos.
5. Send on a Tuesday or Wednesday morning — then follow up once, 5 days later.

One pitch won't change your business. But the habit of pitching — one thoughtful, well-targeted pitch a month — compounds into the kind of steady local presence that advertising budgets struggle to buy.
