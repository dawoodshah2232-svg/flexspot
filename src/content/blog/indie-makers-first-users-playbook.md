Getting your first users is the hardest phase of building anything. The product works, the landing page is live, and… silence. No traffic, no signups, no feedback. Every indie maker hits this wall, and the ones who break through don't do it with a clever growth hack. They do it with a playbook: deliberate, manual, often unglamorous work, sequenced correctly.

This is that playbook — written for indie makers launching real products on real budgets, with zero invented statistics and no "just go viral" hand-waving.

## Step 1: Get your first 10 users by hand

Forget scale. Your first ten users should come from conversations, not channels. This is the oldest advice in startups because it still works: find ten people who have the problem you're solving, talk to them directly, and onboard them personally.

Where to find them:

- **Communities you're already part of.** The Discord, Telegram group, subreddit, or WhatsApp community where you hang out is your first distribution channel. Don't spam it — participate genuinely, then share what you built when it's relevant.
- **Direct outreach.** Message people who complained about the problem online. A thoughtful DM — "I saw your post about X, I built Y to fix it, mind trying it?" — converts far better than any ad at this stage.
- **Your own network.** Friends, former colleagues, fellow makers. The first users of most successful indie products were people the maker already knew.

The goal of the first ten isn't growth — it's feedback. Watch them use the product. Where do they hesitate? What do they misunderstand? Fix those things before you spend a dollar on visibility. A product that confuses its first ten users will confuse ten thousand.

## Step 2: Make your launch a story, not an announcement

An announcement says "my product exists." A story says "here's what I'm building, why, and what happened this week." The second one gets followed; the first one gets scrolled past.

**Building in public** means sharing the journey as it happens: the feature you shipped, the bug that ate your weekend, the first paying user, the pricing change that flopped. Post it where your people are — X/Twitter, Indie Hackers, relevant subreddits, your community of choice.

A few rules that keep it working:

- **Be specific.** "Shipped v0.3" is noise. "Shipped one-click export after 6 users asked for it — here's the before/after" is a story.
- **Be honest.** Share the failures too. Posts about what went wrong consistently outperform posts about what went right, because they're rare and they're useful.
- **Be consistent.** One thread a week beats a burst of ten posts then silence. People follow trajectories, not moments.
- **Always include the link.** Every public update should end with where to try the thing. Attention without a next step evaporates.

Building in public compounds. Each update is a small launch, and the audience that accumulates becomes the launchpad for the real one.

## Step 3: Launch where discovery actually happens

At some point you need to step outside your own audience and put the product where strangers can find it. That's what product discovery platforms are for — places where people go specifically to find new things.

- **The leaderboard is a launch event.** Because ranks move live when boosts land, a coordinated launch-day push creates visible drama — your product climbing past competitors in real time is the kind of thing people screenshot and share.

## Step 4: Turn early fans into distribution

Your first users are your most valuable marketing asset — not because of what they pay, but because of who they reach. A user who loves your product and tells three friends is worth more than any ad campaign you can afford right now.

Make it easy and rewarding for them to spread the word:

- **Give them a link with their name on it.** Referral links work because they're personal. SpotFlex's referral system creates a link like flexspot.lol/s/your-brand?ref=YOUR-CODE with just a name — no signup — and pays 20% commission on resulting payments. Even outside SpotFlex, a simple "give $X, get $X" referral offer outperforms a generic "share this" ask.
- **Give them a mission, not a request.** "Please share" is easy to ignore. "We're trying to overtake [competitor] on the leaderboard today — your boost/share gets us there" is a story they want to be part of.
- **Recognize them publicly.** Thank your early boosters and sharers by name, in public. The Top Referrers board does this automatically on SpotFlex; do the equivalent wherever you launch. Recognition is the cheapest retention tool in existence.
- **Ask for the testimonial while the excitement is fresh.** A sentence from a real early user — used with permission — beats any copy you write yourself.

The pattern: every user should leave with something to share and a reason to share it. Distribution isn't a department; at this stage, it's a feature of how you treat people.

## Step 5: Measure one thing, then iterate

Early-stage analytics should fit on an index card. Pick one metric that represents real usage — not pageviews, not signups, but the action that means someone got value — and watch it weekly.

Then run the loop: ship, launch the update in public, watch the metric, talk to the users who moved it. The makers who win aren't the ones with the best launch day; they're the ones still iterating in month six.

A few honest expectations to carry with you:

- **Launch day is not the peak; it's the starting gun.** Most products get a spike then flatline. The flatline is normal. What matters is whether the baseline creeps up each month.
- **Most channels won't work.** You'll try ten things; two will move the needle. That's not failure, that's the process — the playbook is about finding your two, fast and cheap.
- **Speed beats polish.** A scrappy product launched to real users beats a polished product launched to nobody. You can fix ugly; you can't fix invisible.

## The sequence, on one page

1. Ten users by hand — conversations, communities, direct outreach.
2. Build in public — weekly honest updates, always with a link.
3. Launch on a discovery stage — claim your spot (SpotFlex starts at $1), coordinate a launch push, let fans boost you.
4. Turn fans into distribution — referral links, missions, public recognition.
5. Measure one metric — iterate weekly, keep going.

None of this requires funding, a team, or luck. It requires a product that solves a real problem and the willingness to do unscalable work until the scalable kind kicks in. Your first users are out there — go find them one conversation at a time.
