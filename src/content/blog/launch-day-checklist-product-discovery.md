> **Key takeaways:**
> - Most launch-day disasters are checklist failures, not product failures — broken signup, missing analytics, no support plan.
> - Verify the full user journey in a fresh browser before anyone else sees it.
> - Prepare your listing assets (headline, screenshots, demo) the day before, not the morning of.
> - Have analytics, error tracking, and a support channel live before the traffic arrives.
> - Plan the follow-up before the launch: email capture, feedback form, and a thank-you message for early users.

## Your listing (do this the day before)

### 1. Headline: clear beats clever

Write the one-line description a stranger would understand in three seconds. Test it on someone outside your team — if they can't repeat back what the product does, rewrite it.

### 2. Screenshots and demo: show the happy path

Prepare 3–5 screenshots of the product at its best, plus a short video if you can. Blurry, outdated, or placeholder screenshots are the fastest way to lose a click.

### 3. Your ask is specific

Decide exactly what you want visitors to do — sign up for the beta, try the demo, leave feedback — and say so directly in the listing. One ask, not three.

### 4. Links all work

Click every link in your listing from a logged-out browser. Broken or login-walled links on launch day are embarrassingly common.

## Your website (morning of, before launch)

### 5. The critical path works end to end

In a fresh incognito window, complete the core journey: land → understand → sign up → reach the "aha" moment. If anything breaks, stalls, or confuses you, it will do worse to a stranger.

### 6. Mobile is not broken

A large share of discovery traffic is mobile. Load your site on a real phone, tap through the key flows, and check that text is readable without pinching.

### 7. Loading speed is acceptable

Slow pages kill launches quietly — visitors bounce before they ever see the product. If your hero takes more than a few seconds, optimize images and defer the heavy stuff.

### 8. Contact and social proof exist

An about page, a contact email, and some evidence you're real (testimonials, user count, press mentions — whatever you honestly have). Anonymous products with no contact info convert poorly.

## Your instrumentation (before traffic arrives)

### 9. Analytics is live and tested

Confirm your analytics fires on page views and key events. There's no retroactive data — if tracking isn't live when the spike hits, those insights are gone forever.

### 10. Error tracking is on

Have error monitoring running so you see crashes before users report them. Launch-day traffic finds bugs your testing never did.

### 11. Uptime monitoring is set

A simple uptime check with alerts to your phone. If the site goes down under launch traffic, you want to know in minutes, not from an angry comment hours later.

## Your human systems (the part everyone skips)

### 12. Someone owns support all day

Decide who's watching the inbox, chat, and comments during launch. A response within the hour turns confused visitors into users; silence turns them into bounce statistics.

### 13. Your answers to the obvious questions are written

Pricing, "how is this different from X", "is there a free plan", data privacy — draft honest answers now so you're not composing them under pressure at midnight.

### 14. The team knows the plan

Everyone involved knows the launch time, their role, and the escalation path if something breaks. A five-minute alignment call beats a chaotic group chat.

## After the spike (plan it now)

### 15. Capture and follow-up are ready

Email signup for non-converters, a feedback form for users, and a thank-you note scheduled for day-one supporters. The launch is a moment; what you capture from it is the asset.

---

Print this, work through it the day before, and sleep well on launch night. The products that "launch smoothly" aren't lucky — they're checklist people.
