> **Key takeaways:**
> - A referral contest is a sprint, not a program: 7 days, one prize, one action, one leaderboard.
> - Prizes should be vivid, winnable, and fast — people share what they can picture themselves winning soon.
> - Public rankings are the fuel: a live leaderboard turns sharing into a spectator sport.
> - Design against cheating from day one: one credit per person, clear rules, manual review before paying out.
> - FlexSpot's per-visit referral mechanic can power the "support your favorite" layer of your contest.

## Contests vs. programs: why the distinction matters

A referral *program* is always-on infrastructure: steady, boring, effective over months. A referral *contest* is a 7-day sprint: loud, urgent, designed to spike. Most small brands need the sprint first — it creates the burst of sharing that seeds the program, gives you a story to tell, and teaches you what motivates your audience before you commit to permanent rewards.

This playbook is the sprint. Seven days, one prize, one clear action. Here's how to run it.

## Day 0: design the contest (before you announce anything)

### Pick ONE action

Every share, every click, every confused participant dilutes the contest. Pick exactly one action: "share your personal link, and every unique visitor through it counts as one entry." That's it. Don't add "follow us AND tag two friends AND repost" — each extra step halves participation.

### Design a prize people can picture

The prize rules, learned the hard way by everyone who's run one:

- **Vivid beats valuable.** A $50 voucher to a beloved local restaurant outperforms a $200 generic gift card, because people can picture the dinner. Cash is fine; *specific* cash is better.
- **Winnable beats massive.** A prize 50 people could plausibly win drives more sharing than a prize one person will win. Consider 1 big prize + 5 small ones instead of a single jackpot.
- **Fast beats eventual.** "Winner announced Sunday night" beats "winner announced at the end of the quarter." The shorter the gap between sharing and payoff, the harder people share.
- **Relevant beats generic.** The prize should attract *your* customers, not prize-hunters. A bakery giving away an iPad gets 2,000 entries from people who'll never buy bread. A bakery giving away "free croissants for a year" gets 200 entries from future regulars.

(Illustrative example: a neighborhood fitness studio ran "bring-a-friend week" with the prize being 3 months free membership for the top referrer plus a free class pack for everyone who brought 3+ friends. Small prize pool, perfectly relevant audience, packed classes for a month after.)

### Write the rules before you need them

- One entry per unique visitor (decide how you count: unique device, unique email signup, unique visit per day — and state it publicly)
- No self-referrals, no bots, no purchased traffic — disqualification is automatic
- How ties are broken, when winners are announced, how prizes are delivered
- Manual review before any prize goes out — always

Publish the rules on the contest page. Boring? Yes. The thing that saves you from a 2am argument with an angry participant who gamed your vague wording? Also yes.

## Days 1–2: launch loud

**The announcement needs three things:** what to do (one sentence), what you win (one vivid line), and when it ends (a date, creating urgency). Lead with the prize, not the mechanics. "Win 3 months free — just share your link. Ends Sunday." beats a paragraph about entry mechanics every time.

**Seed the first 10 sharers personally.** Message your best customers, your friends, your early supporters directly — don't just post and hope. Contests with early momentum look alive; contests that start at zero entries look dead, and nobody shares a dead contest. Your personal outreach is the starter motor.

**Give sharers something to say.** Most people won't share a bare link — they need words. Provide 2–3 copy-paste messages they can use ("I'm competing to win X from [brand] — takes 10 seconds to help me out: [link]"). Make sharing effortless and the contest spreads; make it awkward and it dies.

## Days 3–5: feed the momentum

This is where contests are won or lost, and where most organizers go quiet. Don't.

- **Publish a live leaderboard.** This is the single highest-leverage element of the whole contest. A public ranking turns private sharing into a public competition — leaders defend, challengers chase, and spectators check back daily. Leaderboards work because humans are wired to watch races. If your platform supports it, this is non-negotiable.
- **Spotlight the middle of the pack.** Everyone features the leader; smart organizers feature #4 through #10, where the race is actually close. "Only 3 visits separate 5th from 3rd!" creates more sharing than "congratulations to our runaway leader."
- **Run a mid-week twist.** On day 4, announce a surprise: double entries for 24 hours, or a new small prize for the best comeback. Novelty re-energizes sharers whose initial enthusiasm faded.

## Days 6–7: close it hard

- **Countdown loudly.** "36 hours left" / "final 12 hours" — urgency is the last fuel in the tank. Remind, don't spam: two well-timed reminders beat six anxious ones.
- **Close cleanly and publicly.** Announce winners where everyone can see, on time, exactly as the rules promised. Late or vague winner announcements poison the well for every future contest.
- **Deliver fast.** Get prizes out within days. Every day of delay converts a delighted winner into a skeptical one — and winners who post about their prize are your best marketing for contest #2.

## The anti-cheating checklist

Assume gaming attempts from day one — not because people are bad, but because prizes attract optimizers:

1. **Cap credits per visitor** (e.g., one per person per day) so one enthusiastic friend can't generate 500 entries alone.
2. **Watch for patterns:** bursts of entries at 3am, visitors with no engagement beyond the landing page, clusters from the same device fingerprints.
3. **Require something real for the entry to count** — an email signup or an actual visit, not just a link click. The higher the friction, the lower the fraud (balance this against participation).
4. **Reserve the right to review** — stated in the rules — and actually review the top 10 before announcing.
5. **Never auto-pay prizes.** Human eyes on the winner list, every time.

## Where FlexSpot fits: the "support your favorite" layer

Here's a natural way to plug a bid-for-attention leaderboard into your contest mechanics. FlexSpot gives every spot a personal referral link, and visits through that link add to the spot's total on the public leaderboard. So instead of (or alongside) counting raw shares, your contest can run on a live, public board:

- Each contestant gets their FlexSpot spot link as their contest link
- Supporters visiting through it push the contestant up the visible rankings
- The leaderboard IS the contest scoreboard — public, real-time, dramatic

The format does your momentum work for you: leaders get the spotlight, challengers get the chase narrative, and spectators — the audience your brand wanted anyway — show up to watch the race. And because entry starts from $1, running the contest layer costs less than the prize itself.

Even if you run the contest entirely on your own site, steal the core insight: **make the scoreboard public.** Private entry counts are bookkeeping; public rankings are entertainment. Entertainment gets shared.

## After the contest: convert the spike

Day 8 is when most brands waste everything the contest built:

1. **Email everyone who participated** — thank them, announce winners, and give non-winners a small consolation (a discount, early access). They shared for you; reward the behavior, not just the winners.
2. **Keep the list.** Every participant is now a warm contact. Add them to your email list (with proper consent, per the rules you published) and treat them like insiders in future launches.
3. **Publish the story.** "217 people shared, here's what happened" — a recap post turns a one-week spike into evergreen content and social proof for the next contest.
4. **Decide: program or another sprint?** If the contest worked, you now know what motivates your audience. Either launch the always-on [referral program](/blog/how-to-start-a-referral-program) with proven rewards, or schedule contest #2 in 6–8 weeks. Don't let the momentum fully die.

A one-week contest, run cleanly, does three jobs at once: it acquires customers, teaches you your audience's motivations, and gives you a story. That's a good week.
