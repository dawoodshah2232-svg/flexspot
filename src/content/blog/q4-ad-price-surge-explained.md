## The calendar tax is back

There is a week in late September every year when small advertisers open their ad accounts and stare. The campaigns haven't changed. The audiences haven't changed. But the same impressions that cost X on Tuesday suddenly cost 1.3X by Friday. Nothing is broken. It's just Q4, and the auction just got crowded.

It works like this: most digital advertising is sold in auctions. Auction supply — the number of people scrolling, watching, and reading — is roughly fixed. But demand swings wildly with the calendar. Industry analysts estimate that brands spend 50 to 60 percent of their annual advertising budget inside a two-to-three-month window at the end of the year. The same pool of attention gets bid on by everyone at once, and auction math does the rest: when five advertisers become fifty, the clearing price goes up. Nobody has to "raise prices." The auction raises them.

## What the data actually says

AdRoll's Q4 2025 State of Digital Advertising Report, released in December 2025, put hard numbers on the surge. Across more than 20,000 online businesses, display retargeting CPMs climbed 11% year-over-year from September through November, while prospecting CPMs rose 2%. Part of the story was timing: the 2025 holiday shopping season started nearly two months early, with brands pulling promotions forward and shoppers buying earlier. The demand spike didn't just get taller — it got wider.

The pattern isn't new and it isn't small. Marketing analysis firm Webtopia calls it "Q4 CPM inflation" and is blunt about the mechanism: inventory is broadly fixed while demand for it rises sharply, so the auction's clearing price goes up. It is a competition effect, not a platform pricing change, and it reverses in January. If you've been advertising long enough, you've lived the January version: same budget, suddenly more reach, and the brief illusion that your campaigns got smarter overnight.

The 2026 festive season looks no gentler. Indian quick-commerce advertising — where discovery and purchase happen in the same breath — saw keyword costs jump from around Rs 60 to Rs 240 per click heading into the festival window, a fourfold rise. Industry estimates put CPM increases of 20 to 40 percent across channels this season. And 2026 adds a twist in the US: it's a midterm election year, and political advertisers are bidding on the same connected-TV and social inventory as holiday brands, layering extra competition onto an already expensive quarter.

## The part you can't see

Here's what makes the surge dangerous for small budgets: in most programmatic auctions, you can't see the whole machine. You see your bid, your CPM, and your results. You don't see floors, fees, or interventions.

The FTC's 2026 antitrust complaint against Amazon is a case study in why that matters. According to the complaint, Amazon used a system of "soft reserve" prices — which some Amazon employees themselves called "post-hoc pricing adjustments" — intervening in ad auctions 70 to 80 percent of the time to raise the price advertisers paid, with a higher surcharge applied during the Christmas holiday season and other peak shopping events. Amazon disputed the characterization, saying roughly 92% of placed ads aren't given to the highest bid anyway and that its practices didn't harm advertisers or consumers. The case will settle or not on its merits. But the allegation lands because the mechanics are plausible: in an auction you can't observe, the house can quietly adjust the rules — and peak season is exactly when the adjustments are worth the most.

That is the quiet tax inside Q4. Small advertisers don't just pay more because of competition; they pay more inside machines where nobody can audit the final number.

## Higher prices, better buyers

Before you panic and pause everything until February, consider the other half of the equation. A thirty percent rise in media cost is fully offset by a thirty percent improvement in the combination of click-through rate and conversion rate — both of which you control. And Q4 brings something that partially pays for itself: the people seeing your ads in late November are further along in their buying intent than the people seeing them in September. Conversion rates typically rise during the exact period that CPMs do.

That's why so many advertisers run their most expensive media and their best cost-per-purchase in the same week. The number that decides whether the quarter works is contribution margin per order — what each sale actually contributes after all costs — not CPM. Judging the quarter on CPM alone leads brands to pull back from spend that was working. Expensive impressions that convert can be cheap. Cheap impressions that don't convert are the most expensive kind.

## The small-budget playbook

So what does a small brand actually do, facing the most expensive advertising quarter of the year?

**Don't fight mega-brands in their own auctions.** In October through December, you're bidding against companies with budgets measured in millions and agencies that optimize every hour. The same impression you could win cheaply in February now costs you 40% more — and teaches you nothing, because you can't tell which part of the price was competition and which was the machine. Spend your auction dollars where the competition is thinner, or spend them differently.

**Build your retargeting lists before the surge.** Retargeting got hit hardest in the data — that 11% jump. The brands with the biggest high-intent audiences before Q4 were bidding on people they already knew. If your lists are small now, fix that in September; it compounds all quarter.

**Lean on fixed-price visibility.** This is the part of the calendar where auctions are at their worst for small budgets, which is exactly when fixed, known prices win. A placement with a fixed price in October costs the same as in April — no surge, no floor games, no guessing. **Measure margin, not reach.** Decide your contribution margin per order before you spend a rupee, and kill anything that can't beat it — no matter how "premium" the quarter feels. The brands that survive Q4 aren't the ones with the biggest budgets. They're the ones that know their number and hold it.

**Plan the January reset.** Every year, the surge reverses. January inventory is the same attention pool with half the competition. The small brands that win are the ones that enter January with lists, creative, and data — built during the quarter everyone else spent simply surviving.
