> **Key takeaways:**
> - Treat your first $100 as tuition, not investment: its job is to teach you what works, not to transform your business overnight.
> - Spend nothing on ads until your landing page is ready to convert — otherwise you're paying to learn that your page leaks.
> - Concentrate the budget: one channel, one offer, one landing page. Splitting $100 five ways teaches you nothing.
> - Reserve part of the budget for the unglamorous basics (a domain, decent photos, review generation) that multiply every ad dollar.

## The mindset: tuition, not lottery

The fastest way to waste your first $100 is expecting it to change your business. It won't — and that's fine. Its real job is to buy you **information**: which message gets clicks, which offer gets conversions, what a customer actually costs you to acquire. Businesses that treat the first $100 as an experiment make smarter $1,000 decisions later. Businesses that treat it as a lottery ticket usually conclude "advertising doesn't work" and quit.

Before spending anything, read our [dollar-a-day breakdown](/blog/dollar-a-day-advertising-what-it-buys) to calibrate expectations, and make sure your site is ready to [turn visitors into customers](/blog/how-to-turn-website-traffic-into-customers) — sending paid traffic to a page that doesn't convert is the most common way small budgets die.

## Step 0: Spend $0 first (the free groundwork)

Seriously. Before the $100, do the free work that multiplies it:

- **Claim and complete your free listings.** Google Business Profile, local directories, relevant marketplaces. These cost nothing and capture people already looking.
- **Fix your landing page basics.** Clear headline, one call to action, contact details, a few reviews. (See the conversion guide above.)
- **Set up measurement.** Install analytics and the ad platforms' pixels now, even before you run ads — data collection starts the day you install, not the day you spend.
- **Ask for reviews.** Message your happiest past customers and ask for a public review. Five genuine reviews beat $100 of ads for trust.

If you skipped these, your $100 is subsidizing fixable weaknesses. Do the free work first.

## The $100 spending plan

Here's one sensible allocation. Adjust the ratios to your situation, but keep the principle: **concentrate, don't scatter.**

### $40 — One paid test on one platform

Pick the platform matching how your customers buy ([Facebook vs Google guide](/blog/facebook-ads-vs-google-ads-on-a-small-budget)). Run a single simple campaign for 2–3 weeks:

- **If Google:** a tight search campaign on your 5–10 highest-intent keywords, geo-limited to where you serve.
- **If Meta:** one ad set, one clear offer, your best photo or a simple phone-shot video.

Set a daily cap, check it every few days, and kill what clearly isn't working. The goal isn't profit yet — it's learning your cost per click and cost per conversion.

### $25 — Creative and trust assets

Ads amplify what exists; they can't fix a business that looks untrustworthy:

- Decent product or shop photos (even a budget phone-photography session, or paying a local student photographer).
- A simple logo touch-up or a clean social profile banner if yours looks abandoned.
- Printed QR codes linking to your review page, placed where customers see them.

These assets keep working long after the $100 is gone.

### $20 — A visibility experiment outside the duopoly

Put a small amount somewhere unconventional to compare:

- A **$1+ bid for a featured homepage spot** on a leaderboard-style placement ([how bid-for-attention works](/blog/what-is-bid-for-attention-marketing)) — tests whether placement-based visibility drives curious visitors for pocket change.
- A sponsored post in a local community group or newsletter.
- Flyers or a small local print run if you're a neighborhood business.

The point is comparison: you'll learn whether alternative channels beat the giants for your niche.

### $15 — Reserve for doubling down

Don't spend this upfront. After 2–3 weeks, put it into whatever showed the first signs of life — the ad with the best click-through, the creative people responded to, the channel with the cheapest engagement. This teaches the most important advertising skill: **reallocating toward evidence.**

## What NOT to spend it on

- **Boosting random posts** with no landing page or offer behind them. Vanity metrics, zero learning.
- **Buying followers, likes, or bulk traffic.** Fake engagement that teaches nothing and can harm your accounts ([why buying traffic is usually a trap](/blog/should-you-buy-website-traffic)).
- **Expensive tools and subscriptions** you don't need yet. Free analytics and free design tools are enough at this stage.
- **A fancy video you can't iterate on.** One polished video you can't afford to remake beats nothing, but three rough phone videos you can test and improve beat one polished one.
- **Spreading $20 across five platforms.** Below a minimum threshold, no channel gets enough data to tell you anything.

## How to know if the $100 worked

Judge the experiment on learning, not revenue:

1. **Do you know your cost per click** on your chosen platform now? (Baseline established.)
2. **Do you know your conversion rate** from ad click to inquiry or sale?
3. **Did one message or creative clearly beat the others?** (A winner to scale.)
4. **Did anything surprise you?** (Unexpected audience, odd search terms, a channel outperforming — surprises are the most valuable output.)

If you can answer these, the $100 bought something worth far more than $100: a grounded starting point for the next $500. If you can't, the money went to activity without measurement — fix tracking before spending more.

## Two sample $100 plans

**Plan A: Local service business (plumber, salon, tutor).** $50 into a tight Google search campaign on your highest-intent local keywords for two weeks. $25 into review generation assets: a printed QR code stand for your counter linking to your review page, plus thank-you cards with the QR. $15 into decent before/after photos (good lighting, clean framing — shot on a phone is fine). $10 reserve to extend whatever keyword or ad showed life. Expected learning: your cost per inquiry and which services people actually search for.

**Plan B: Online store (niche products).** $40 into Meta ads: one campaign, your best product photo or a 15-second phone video, a clear introductory offer. $25 into product photography improvements (a simple lightbox setup or a half-day with a local photographer). $20 into a micro-visibility experiment: a $1+ [featured homepage bid](/blog/what-is-bid-for-attention-marketing) plus a sponsored post in a niche community. $15 reserve for the winner. Expected learning: which product and creative gets clicks, and whether alternative placements beat the duopoly for your niche.

Both plans follow the same discipline: one main test, supporting assets that outlast the spend, one experiment outside the obvious, and a reserve for evidence-based doubling down.

## After the first $100

The businesses that grow from here do three things: they keep the winner running, they reinvest early revenue into the proven channel, and they start building the free traffic engines ([SEO and content](/blog/free-ways-to-get-website-traffic)) that will eventually reduce their dependence on paid. Paid advertising bought you speed and information. Now compound it.

## Tracking your $100: the simple ledger

Keep a one-page record of the experiment — future you will thank present you:

| Spend | Channel | Result | Learning |
|---|---|---|---|
| $40 | Google/Meta test | X clicks, Y inquiries | Cost per inquiry = $Z |
| $25 | Creative assets | Photos, review QR codes | Reusable indefinitely |
| $20 | Placement experiment | X visitors | Beat/matched the duopoly? |
| $15 | Reserve → winner | Extended best performer | Confirmed scaling candidate |

One page, updated weekly during the test. When the next $500 arrives, you won't be guessing — you'll have a baseline, a winner, and a plan. That's what $100 of tuition buys when you treat it as tuition.
